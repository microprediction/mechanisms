Local (Hyvärinen) scoring rules

The log score needs a normalized density: you must know the partition function $Z$. A local proper scoring rule does not. It depends on the forecast only through the derivatives of $\log p$ at the realised outcome, and since $\log p = \log\tilde p - \log Z$, every derivative drops the constant. An energy-based or un-normalised model can therefore be scored directly, no partition function in sight.

CRPS does not belong in that first sentence, and this page used to put it there. CRPS needs no density at all: it is defined by $\int (F(z) - \mathbf 1\{y \le z\})^2\,dz$ on all Borel probability measures on $\mathbb R$, which is exactly why it handles a point mass at zero in a precipitation forecast, or a bag of MCMC samples, where a density-based score cannot go (Gneiting & Raftery 2007, §4.2). A finite first moment buys strict propriety, not propriety. What CRPS is not is local: it reads the whole distribution function, and the property it does have is sensitivity to distance (Epstein 1969; Staël von Holstein 1970).

What "m-local" means

A scoring rule is $m$-local if it depends on the quoted density only through its value and its first $m$ derivatives at the outcome $y$. Parry, Dawid & Lauritzen (2012) proved that genuinely local proper rules on the real line exist for every even $m$ and no odd $m$: there is no proper $1$-local or $3$-local rule, but there are proper rules at every even order, trading more derivative information for more modelling freedom while staying free of the normalizing constant.

Order $0$ is where this page was wrong. "Ordinary proper rules are $0$-local" is false. Up to $a(x) - \lambda\log p(x)$ with $\lambda > 0$, the logarithmic score is the only one — that is the classical locality theorem (Bernardo 1979 in the continuous case; Dawid, Lauritzen & Parry 2012 §3.2 in the discrete). Brier, spherical, pseudospherical, CRPS and the energy score are all proper and none of them is $0$-local. Brier depends on $\sum_j p_j^2$, so the forecasts $(0.5, 0.25, 0.25)$ and $(0.5, 0.5, 0)$ assign the same probability to outcome 1 and score differently, which is precisely what $0$-locality forbids.

The discrete theorem carries a hypothesis that is easy to drop and matters here: more than two outcomes. With two, $p_0 = 1 - p_1$, so knowing the probability of the realised outcome already determines the entire forecast. Locality constrains nothing, and every proper binary rule is trivially local. The theorem is not merely tight at two outcomes; it is vacuous there.

The Hyvärinen score ($m = 2$)

The canonical local rule is the Hyvärinen score, in loss form (lower is better):

$S(y, p) = \Delta \log p(y) + \tfrac12\,\lVert \nabla \log p(y)\rVert^2.$

Its population minimiser is score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005), and the divergence it induces is the Fisher divergence

$D_F(p_{\text{data}}, p) = \tfrac12\!\int p_{\text{data}}\,\lVert \nabla\log p - \nabla\log p_{\text{data}}\rVert^2,$

always non-negative, and for a Gaussian $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ the score has the closed form $S = (y-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^4) - 1/\sigma^2$.

Strict propriety, though, needs hypotheses, and one of them is invisible in the usual statements. Hyvärinen's Theorem 2 assumes a strictly positive model density, differentiability, finite expectations of the squared scores, and a vanishing tail $p(\xi)\psi(\xi)\to 0$. Its proof then goes: $D_F = 0$ forces $\nabla\log p = \nabla\log q$ pointwise, hence $\log p = \log q + c$, hence $c = 0$ because both integrate to one. The middle step is a connectedness argument, and neither Hyvärinen nor Parry, Dawid & Lauritzen names it — the latter are protected only by their standing choice of an interval or $\mathbb R^k$ as the sample space.

On a disconnected support it fails outright. Equal gradients make the log difference constant on each connected component, so with $k$ components there are $k$ constants and still one normalisation equation, leaving $k-1$ mixing weights entirely unidentified. Put half the mass on each of two smooth bumps on disjoint intervals, then reweight to $(0.01, 0.99)$: the Fisher divergence stays at $10^{-24}$, numerically zero, while the total variation distance is $0.49$ and the KL divergence is $1.61$. The bumps are $C^\infty$ with compact support, so every boundary term in the integration by parts vanishes exactly and this is not a boundary artefact. The Hyvärinen score simply cannot see the relative mass of components it cannot travel between (Wenliang et al. 2019, §3.1; Song & Ermon 2019, §3.2.2, where it is the motivation for annealed Langevin sampling).

Why dropping $Z$ matters

In high-frequency forecasting, forcing every agent to compute a normalizing constant for its predictive density is a real tax, often the dominant cost for flexible models. A local rule lets a lightweight agent submit a complex, unnormalised density (a mixture or energy model written without its $Z$) and be scored faithfully. The trade-off: local rules reward getting the shape of $\log p$ right near the outcome and are correspondingly less sensitive to far-tail mass than the log score.

In this repo

mechanisms/local_scoring.py provides hyvarinen_score (from analytic $\nabla\log p$ and $\Delta\log p$), hyvarinen_score_fd (finite-difference, for any un-normalised log_pdf callable), gaussian_hyvarinen_score (closed form), and fisher_divergence_gaussian. The demo examples/sim_local_scoring.py ranks several un-normalised models against a true stream, recovering the right order, and confirms the scores are invariant to an arbitrary additive log-constant.

Try it

The Hyvärinen (local) score of a Gaussian quote $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ at an outcome $y$: $S=(y-\mu)^2/2\sigma^4 - 1/\sigma^2$. It uses only derivatives of $\log p$, so it never needs the normalising constant (loss form, lower is better).
